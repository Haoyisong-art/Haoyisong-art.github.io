Artist Statement

My work investigates how women's bodies become sites of emotional governance, where pain, discipline, and transformation are moralized and socially coded. I use everyday objects—beds, chairs, pillows, garments—as metaphors for endurance, containment, and relational spaces. Painting is integral to my practice: through transparency, erasure, repetition, and layered surfaces, I allow the canvas to become an archive, a memory, and a testimony. At the same time, I explore how different media articulate meaning in distinct ways. Grounded in cross-cultural perspectives, my art highlights both shared human experiences and cultural specificities in how bodies, identities, and emotional regimes are constructed.

VPAS Doctoral Program, University of Texas at Dallas

My doctoral project, Weaving and Unweaving Order: How Soft Materials Shape and Resist the Governance of Women's Bodies, investigates how soft materials, from historical textiles to contemporary art installations, function as a primary medium for the affective governance of the female body. My central argument is that materials like thread, cloth, and paper are not passive objects but are active technologies of feeling and control, used to enact and resist ideological norms.

This research bridges material culture studies and affect theory to explore how the physical properties of softness (plasticity, intimacy, wearability) and the techniques applied to them (stitching, weaving, binding) shape the sentient body. The project is guided by a central question: How have soft materials been used to discipline women's bodies, and how do contemporary female artists use these same materials to reclaim or re-narrate this history?

The dissertation is structured around this inquiry:

  • It first establishes the historical connection between softness, yin, and femininity, analyzing how materials and domestic space are gendered.
  • It then examines traditional textile practices like embroidery, not just as craft, but as a form of discipline and affective regulation, where the repetitive labor and physical confinement recode pain and endurance into moral virtue.
  • Finally, it analyzes how contemporary artists, both within China and in the diaspora, engage with these material legacies to explore themes of memory, trauma, and identity, positioning their work, and my own studio practice, as a form of practice-as-research.
